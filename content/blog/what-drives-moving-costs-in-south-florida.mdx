People usually ask "how much does a two-bedroom cost?" and the honest answer is that the bedroom count is one of the weaker predictors. Two identical two-bedroom apartments can differ by 40% depending on what floor they are on and where the truck can park.

Here is what actually moves the number.

## Access, which nobody thinks about

Access is the single biggest swing factor on a local move, and it is almost entirely invisible in an online quote form.

**The carry.** The distance from your door to where the truck can legally park. Under about 75 feet is normal. A long carry — a rear building in a complex, a beachfront condo where the loading dock is on the far side, a house on a street where a 26-footer cannot turn — adds hours and often an accessorial charge.

**Stairs.** Every flight costs time on every single item. A third-floor walk-up can add 30–50% to a local job. This is why we ask about it during the estimate rather than discovering it on the day.

**Elevators.** A reserved service elevator is fine. A shared passenger elevator in a busy building is brutal, because the crew waits. Buildings that only allow moves in a four-hour window compress everything, which means we staff heavier to finish in time.

**Shuttles.** If a full-size truck genuinely cannot reach the property — common in Coral Gables, Coconut Grove, and parts of old Fort Lauderdale — we run a smaller shuttle between the house and the trailer. It is the most expensive access problem there is, so it is worth establishing early whether you have one.

## Volume, not square footage

Movers price on cubic feet and weight, not on floor area. A minimalist in a 2,000-square-foot house can be a smaller job than a collector in a one-bedroom.

The reliable predictors are:

- **Number of cartons.** Roughly 20 for a studio, 30 for a one-bedroom, 45 for a two-bedroom, 65+ for a three-bedroom house.
- **Large furniture count.** Sectionals, armoires, treadmills, and anything that needs two people minimum.
- **The garage, the attic, and the shed.** This is where estimates go wrong. Every mover has a story about a garage that added four hours. A good estimator walks all three.

## Timing, which you may partly control

Moving is intensely seasonal, and South Florida has two overlapping cycles.

**The national cycle:** late May through Labor Day is peak. Prices are highest and availability lowest.

**The Florida cycle:** October–November is heavy inbound and April–May is heavy outbound as seasonal residents come and go. In Palm Beach County especially, those months book out before summer does.

Within any month, **the first and last three days are the most expensive**, because leases turn over. Mid-month, mid-week is the cheapest window there is — often 15–25% below a month-end Saturday for identical work.

If your dates are flexible, that flexibility is worth real money. Say so when you get quoted.

## Services, priced separately

- **Packing.** Full packing typically adds a crew-day plus materials. Partial or fragile-only is much cheaper and covers most of the actual risk.
- **Materials.** Cartons, paper, tape, wardrobe boxes, mattress bags. Ask whether they are included or billed — practice varies.
- **Specialty items.** Pianos, safes over about 600 lbs, slate pool tables, marble, and large art each carry their own charge because they need specific equipment and extra crew.
- **Storage.** Priced monthly by volume. Climate control costs more and, in Florida, is not really optional for anything wooden or upholstered.
- **Valuation.** Full-value protection is an add-on. Released value is free and covers 60 cents per pound, which is not coverage in any meaningful sense.

## Long distance is a different formula

Interstate moves are priced on **weight and distance**, plus accessorials. Two structural choices dominate the cost:

**Dedicated vs consolidated.** A dedicated truck carries only your shipment and delivers in one to three days. Consolidated freight shares the truck with other households and can take two weeks. Consolidated is cheaper per pound; dedicated is what you want if you cannot camp in an empty house.

**Shuttle at either end.** If a tractor-trailer cannot reach one of the addresses, that is an extra transfer, and it is billed.

## What this means practically

If you want the cheapest honest number:

1. Move mid-month and mid-week if you possibly can.
2. Get rid of things before the estimate, not after. You pay to move volume.
3. Tell the estimator about the stairs, the long carry, and the garage. Hiding them does not make them cheaper — it just moves the charge to move day, when you have no leverage.
4. Ask about hourly versus flat rate. Hourly usually wins for small, easy-access jobs; flat rate protects you when the clock is outside your control.
5. Take the fragile-only pack even if you skip the rest.

And get it in writing, binding, against a walked inventory. A cheap non-binding estimate is not a price. It is an opening position.
